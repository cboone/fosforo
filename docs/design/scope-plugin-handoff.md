# Handoff: A Mac-First Signal-Analysis Plugin in Zig / CLAP / Metal

## What this document is

This is a faithful record of an extended brainstorming conversation about building an audio signal-analysis plugin — think "oscilloscope and everything adjacent to it" — as a Mac-first, single-developer project written in Zig, authored as a CLAP plugin, and rendered with Metal. It is deliberately **not** a specification. It makes no final feature decisions, picks no single product out of the many possibilities discussed, and contains no implementation code. Its job is to hand the next session the entire territory we mapped — the technical stack and its rationale, the rendering approach, and the full sprawling array of monitor and analyzer concepts we generated — so that the next session can do its own additional research, resolve the open choices, and produce a phased build plan.

Two framing instructions for the reader that follow from how this material was produced:

First, where the conversation established *reasoning* (for example, why CLAP rather than authoring an Audio Unit directly, or why a WebView is the wrong rendering substrate for this particular kind of plugin), that reasoning is preserved here because it is load-bearing and will keep the next session from re-litigating settled ground. Where the conversation surfaced *options* (for example, how to handle eventual cross-platform portability, or which of many analyzer representations to build), those are preserved as options, not collapsed into decisions. The planning session should treat the reasoning as context and the options as a menu.

Second, the functionality section is intentionally broad and unsynthesized. The whole point of that part of the brainstorm was to resist prematurely narrowing "an oscilloscope" into one tidy product, and instead to lay out both the faithful/traditional monitors and a set of less conventional framings that escape the assumptions inherited from physical hardware. Do not read the breadth as a mandate to build all of it. Read it as the design space to choose from.

## Part 1 — Project motivation

The project exists because of a specific and well-defined gap in the plugin market. Across the categories of tools a producer uses, most have acquired modern, resizable, legible interfaces over the last decade — synthesizers, EQs, compressors, limiters, and creative effects have all seen a general uplift in interaction design. Metering and *analysis* tools have not. The moment a producer wants something beyond the spectrum analyzer that vendors fold into their EQs — a proper oscilloscope, a phase-correlation display, a stereo goniometer, a long-window spectrogram — they fall back to tools whose interfaces and rendering quality are effectively frozen in the Win32 era. The free spectrum analyzer that serves as a de-facto standard looks its age; the most powerful analyzer suites are nearly unreadable; the oscilloscopes people still recommend date to the early 2000s.

The reason this gap exists is economic rather than technical, and understanding that is part of the project's premise. Producers pay for tools that *change the sound*. Analysis tools do not change the sound, and the category has a price ceiling set by capable free options, so no vendor can justify a multi-year design investment in, say, a beautiful oscilloscope. The DSP underneath these tools is nearly trivial — triggering, decimation, correlation, a vectorscope, an FFT are all undergraduate signal processing. The entire moat for a genuinely excellent analyzer would be *rendering and interaction design*, which is exactly the kind of work the market has declined to fund. That is the opening: the gap is a market failure, not a hard problem, and it is unusually well-scoped for a single motivated developer.

This gap is especially acute for the low-frequency, kick-versus-bass alignment work central to genres built on a tight kick-bass interlock (the conversation's running example was progressive psytrance, but the point generalizes to any bass-forward electronic music). Aligning a kick against a bass where they overlap in the low end is fundamentally a waveform-domain and phase-domain problem, and the tooling for looking at that clearly is genuinely poor. This use case recurs throughout the functionality section as a concrete motivating scenario, and it is worth keeping in mind as the throughline that ties many of the otherwise-disparate monitor ideas together.

## Part 2 — Technical stack and architecture

### Platform posture: Mac-first, Apple Silicon

The project is Mac-first by deliberate choice, and for a single developer this is a simplification rather than a limitation. Committing to one platform and one vendor collapses a large lattice of conditional decisions into constants, and the simplifications compound (they are enumerated in their own section below). "Mac-first" here specifically contemplates Apple Silicon as the primary target, which unlocks further simplifications around SIMD and memory architecture. Whether to also ship an Intel-Mac binary is one of the few axes of variation worth retaining and is left open.

A note on precedent: the boutique end of the plugin market is substantially populated by Mac-first, single-developer or tiny-team shops that build against Apple's audio frameworks directly because that is the platform their author lives on. The archetype at the extreme is Airwindows — modular, deliberately *graphic-less* Audio Unit plugins for the Mac, which is the maximal statement of "the UI is not the product" and, not coincidentally, sidesteps the rendering question entirely by having no custom UI at all. This project sits at the opposite pole from Airwindows on the interface question (the rendering *is* the product here) but in the same Mac-native, format-native tradition. Mac-only-forever has become somewhat rarer because wrapper tooling made "also ship Windows" cheap, but Mac-*first* — where the Mac build is the real product and anything else is downstream — remains the default posture of the small end of this market, and it is a well-precedented place to stand.

### Language: Zig

The language is Zig, and the single most important reason is its frictionless C interoperability, which aligns almost perfectly with the plugin format choice below. Zig can consume a C header directly with no binding layer to write or maintain and no code generation step. This turns out to be the deciding advantage once the format is CLAP, because CLAP is a plain C API. Zig also brings two further properties that fit this problem domain unusually well: portable first-class SIMD via its vector types, and an explicit-allocator convention that makes real-time-safety something the code structure can help enforce rather than merely a discipline one hopes was maintained. Both are expanded below.

The cost side of Zig is real and must be planned around: the language is pre-1.0 and moves, so the project should pin a specific compiler version and expect to occasionally spend effort on compiler-version churn that has nothing to do with audio.

### Plugin format: author CLAP, project outward with clap-wrapper

The plugin is authored **once**, as a CLAP, and only as a CLAP. This is the pivotal architectural decision and the reasoning behind it should be treated as settled.

CLAP is a plain C header, MIT-licensed, with no SDK license agreement and no C++ in its contract. Any language with a C FFI can implement it directly; in Zig it is not so much "supported" as simply *there* via direct C import. This is qualitatively different from the alternatives. VST3 is not a C API at all — it is a COM-style C++ interface with vtable layout and reference counting baked into the binary contract, which any non-C++ language has to hand-reconstruct, making VST3 bindings the most fragile part of any non-C++ stack. Audio Units are C-callable at the entry point but sit atop a substantial pile of Core Audio and Objective-C plumbing. CLAP is the one modern format whose design lets you both author it cleanly from Zig and, crucially, have a wrapper fan it out to every other format.

That fan-out is the second half of the plan. The plugin does not target Audio Units directly. Instead, `clap-wrapper` (the free-audio project) is used to *project* the authored CLAP into the other formats. The mental model that matters: clap-wrapper is not a port or a rewrite or a second target you maintain — it is itself a small CLAP host wearing the costume of whatever format the outer host wants. An Audio Unit host loads the wrapper; the wrapper is handed your `clap_entry`; it translates every interaction — parameters, audio buffers, GUI parent-window handoff, state save/load — between the outer format's interface and your inner CLAP, in both directions. Your plugin never knows it is not talking to a native CLAP host. The set of formats it can project into is the full one: CLAP-native, Audio Unit v2, Audio Unit v3 (macOS and iOS), VST3, AAX, and a standalone application — all from the same unchanged source. The absorbing of all the C++ (VST3) and Objective-C (AU) plumbing is the wrapper's problem, written and maintained once by that project, not yours.

The one important constraint this navigates: **Logic loads only Audio Units.** A raw `.clap` is invisible in Logic. So the practical development loop is to keep and develop against the raw CLAP — because a CLAP-native host (Reaper was the named example) loads it instantly, and `clap-validator` tests it with no wrapper in the path, so you are debugging the clean thing — and then run clap-wrapper to *also* emit the Audio Unit that Logic needs, validated with `auval`. VST3, AAX, and standalone remain future toggles, not things to build now.

The limits of the projection approach, for accuracy: the projection is only as complete as the feature mapping, so any plugin leaning on CLAP-specific capabilities (polyphonic per-note modulation, the threadpool extension, and so on) would find those degraded or absent once projected into an older format. An analyzer — audio in, audio out, a Cocoa GUI, perhaps a few parameters — maps completely and cleanly, so this is a non-issue for this specific project, but it is the general boundary of the technique. Also, clap-wrapper is a real build-system component to configure (a little CMake glue to invoke it from a Zig project), not a magic post-processing pass — an afternoon of build plumbing, not an ongoing tax.

### AUv2 versus AUv3: a packaging decision, not a quality one

A distinction worth carrying into planning: AU-only and AUv3-only are different commitments. The default path above targets AUv2 (the classic `.component`), which is right for reach because every Mac DAW loads it. AUv3 is the modern rewrite, delivered as an *app extension* rather than a bare dynamic library, and everything that differs between the two flows from that one architectural fact.

What AUv3 would buy: reach into the whole Apple ecosystem, since the same extension runs on iOS/iPadOS (the only path to an iPad version, which for a touch-manipulable visual instrument is a genuinely appealing future); process isolation, because an app extension runs out-of-process from the host, so a crash takes down the extension rather than the user's session (a real robustness gain for live GPU code specifically); alignment with the format Apple is actively investing in while AUv2 is legacy-maintained; and a Mac App Store distribution path. What it costs: more elaborate packaging (container app, entitlements, sandbox), a cross-process compositing path for the GUI, and — paradoxically — *narrower* desktop host compatibility than AUv2, since some hosts are fussier about AUv3.

The crucial clarification, which should be stated plainly to avoid a common misunderstanding: **AUv3 offers no audio-quality or visual-quality improvement over AUv2.** The format difference lives entirely in the packaging-and-lifecycle layer — where the plugin runs, how it is delivered, how it fails — and not in the signal path or the rendering path. Audio quality is a function of your DSP, your numeric precision, and the buffers the host feeds you, all of which are identical across formats because they are your own code reached through a different front door. Visual quality is a function of Metal and your shaders, which neither format touches. If anything, AUv3's out-of-process model puts one extra boundary in both the audio path (shared-memory transport, well-mitigated by Apple) and the GUI compositing path, so any architectural lean on *output* quality tilts very slightly toward in-process AUv2, not AUv3. The upshot is liberating for planning: because output quality is entirely in the layer you control and share across all formats, the format choice can be made purely on packaging merits with zero worry about compromising sound or pixels. Ship the easy-to-load AUv2 first; treat AUv3 as a later toggle (clap-wrapper can emit both) flipped if and when iPad reach, crash isolation, or the App Store becomes worth its packaging cost.

### Rendering: Metal, and the portability seam

Rendering is done with Metal. Metal is Apple-only with no caveats — there is no Metal on Windows or Linux — so the literal rendering code locks *that layer* to macOS. But the scope of the lock is narrower than it feels, and this distinction should be carried forward carefully.

Everything below the renderer is platform-neutral by construction: CLAP is cross-platform, the DSP is pure computation that compiles identically anywhere Zig targets (the vector kernels lower to NEON on Apple Silicon or AVX on x86 with no source change), the lock-free buffer is just atomics and an array, and even the *structure* of the renderer (a persistent floating-point accumulation texture, a decay pass, an additively-blended weighted trace, a tonemap) is an abstract GPU algorithm any modern API expresses. What is Apple-specific is a thin skin: the concrete calls that allocate the texture, the shading language the passes are written in, and the windowing glue that hands a drawable to the host. So "Metal locks me to macOS" is true of the renderer's *implementation* but not of the *plugin*, and the locked-in piece is small.

Whether the lock matters at all depends on a question the project has not yet had to answer: will this ever ship to Windows and Linux users? If it is a tool for the author's own Logic workflow, or a macOS product (a respectable and common posture for boutique audio developers), then Metal-direct is simply the right call — best-integrated, lowest-overhead graphics path, one shading language, no abstraction tax — and there is no portability cost to cash in. If cross-platform is a real future goal, then the decision is not "Metal or not" but *where to put the seam*, and four strategies were identified, to be chosen among later, not now:

- **A small internal rendering interface.** Define a handful of operations the scope actually needs (create a float texture; run a fullscreen pass with a fragment shader; draw N instanced quads with additive blending; present) and write a Metal backend behind it now, a second backend later. The interface is small because it is shaped to this algorithm, so a second backend is bounded work. The discipline is that Metal types must not leak upward past the interface.
- **Adopt a cross-platform GPU abstraction.** `wgpu` (the native WebGPU implementation, with a stable C header callable from Zig via C import, shading in the single-source WGSL) targets Metal/D3D/Vulkan from one backend. The cost is a large dependency and a philosophical tension with the "own the stack" appeal that motivated Zig-plus-Metal in the first place.
- **Author shaders once and cross-compile them.** Write in WGSL or GLSL, compile to SPIR-V as the intermediate, translate to Metal Shading Language and HLSL per platform with the mature SPIR-V toolchain. Solves "author every shader twice" while still hand-writing each host-side backend; more build machinery.
- **The WebView escape hatch** — explicitly rejected for *this* plugin (see below), noted only for completeness.

The recommendation that emerged: build the Metal backend directly and do **not** abstract it yet, but put the seam in from day one as code hygiene — keep the renderer behind that small internal interface and keep Metal types from leaking upward, even with only one backend behind it. That costs almost nothing now and makes an eventual port "write a second backend against a known four-operation interface" rather than "excavate Metal calls out of a renderer they have grown through." Adopting `wgpu` or a shader-cross-compile pipeline pre-emptively, before cross-platform is a known requirement, is the thing to avoid — it pays a concrete tax today against a benefit that may never come due, and dilutes exactly the own-the-stack quality that made the native choice attractive. Design for the port; do not build it until someone needs it.

### Why not a WebView UI

This was examined directly because contemporary plugins increasingly use system WebViews (via the documented JUCE 8 pathway, among others), and it needed ruling in or out. The conclusion for *this* plugin is that a WebView is disqualified on architecture, and the reasoning should be preserved so it is not revisited.

First, to correct the usual mental model: these are not Electron — the WebView is the operating system's own component (WebKit on macOS), not a bundled Chromium, so the "200 MB per plugin" objection does not apply. The real costs are different: each editor spawns an out-of-process web-content process, which falls over at high instance counts; there is a startup-latency/reload cost; and, decisively for an analyzer, the JavaScript-to-native bridge is asynchronous and serializing, with no cheap path to get an audio-rate sample buffer across it. Some objections do *not* hold — a static control panel at 60 Hz is trivial for a compositor, and the stability argument actually runs the *other* way, since the DSP stays in C/native on the audio thread and the WebView living in another process is structurally incapable of causing the classic UI-induced audio dropouts. There is also a real and underdiscussed security surface: the bridge is a scripting-to-native call path inside a process holding the DAW's entitlements, and WebView-choosing plugins tend to be exactly the ones pulling remote content and an npm dependency tree, importing the JavaScript supply-chain threat model into a category with little security-review culture.

But the disqualifying point for a *live measurement instrument* specifically is that the phosphor-style renderer needs a lock-free, audio-rate buffer feeding a GPU pass on a schedule you control, and the WebView's serializing bridge and borrowed compositor schedule are precisely wrong for that. Dragging a cursor along a trace and reading an instantaneous phase value needs input and rendering on the same timeline you own; the moment compositing happens on the WebView's schedule you have introduced variable lag into a measurement tool, defeating its purpose. WebView remains a reasonable trade for a *different*, control-panel-shaped, visualization-light plugin — it is a hiring-and-iteration-speed decision more than a technical one — but not for this.

For context on where the genuinely well-rendered plugins actually sit: they are mostly on a *custom GPU renderer* track, not the web one. FabFilter has always used an in-house framework (part of why their interfaces feel unlike anything else). The notable recent development is **Visage**, the GPU-accelerated, MIT-licensed UI library Matt Tytel extracted from Vital's UI layer — shader-based, using signed distance fields, redrawing only dirty regions. SDF rendering is the right structural answer to the Retina-crispness problem because a shape defined as a distance field is resolution-independent by construction, so resizability becomes free rather than a matter of shipping bitmap assets at several densities. Bitwig went through a comparable GPU rewrite. Visage is worth the next session's attention as prior art (and as a possible reference or even dependency for the "own the view, render with the GPU" approach), precisely because it targets the case where the UI is a live signal display rather than a wall of knobs.

### The threading model

The architecture reduces to three threads that never block each other, and keeping these boundaries clean is what prevents audio dropouts:

- The host's **audio thread** *produces* samples (the plugin's process callback taps the signal here).
- A **display-link thread** (CVDisplayLink) *consumes* the most recent samples to draw, at vsync.
- The host's **main thread** owns the AppKit view lifecycle (creation, resize, parent handoff).

The only thing connecting the first two is a lock-free buffer; the handshake for the third is the CLAP GUI extension. Two rules follow that the planning session must respect: the render thread touches Metal only and must never mutate AppKit state (any such mutation is marshalled to the main thread), and the audio thread must remain real-time-safe (below).

### The lock-free history buffer

A correction worth carrying forward: the buffer between audio and render threads is **not a queue**. A scope wants the most recent *window* and is happy to overwrite everything older, so the right structure is a circular history buffer with a single monotonically increasing write cursor — simpler than and better-suited than a real queue. The producer (audio thread) writes samples and publishes the advanced cursor with release semantics; the consumer (render thread) reads a trailing window relative to an acquire load of that cursor. Because capacity (on the order of a second) vastly exceeds any display window (tens of milliseconds), the producer cannot realistically lap the consumer between publish and copy, so a seqlock-style retry loop is unnecessary. This one structure is the entire concurrency story between the two threads: no lock, no allocation, no room for priority inversion.

### Real-time safety

The audio callback runs on an OS-scheduled thread with hard deadlines and may not allocate, may not take a lock a lower-priority thread can hold, may not make a syscall, and may not be interrupted by a runtime. For an analyzer whose process callback merely passes audio through and taps one channel into the history buffer, there is genuinely almost nothing real-time-unsafe to get wrong — which is part of why an analyzer is a good first project in this stack.

Zig contributes something structural here that is worth exploiting: its explicit-allocator convention can turn real-time-safety into a property of the API surface. If the DSP path takes an allocator parameter and is handed a fixed-buffer allocator sized at preparation time, then "does the audio path touch the heap" becomes a fact about the call graph rather than a convention one hopes was honored. (Note the general caution that applies to any language here, including Rust: memory-safety is not real-time-safety — a lock or an allocation compiles perfectly and still destroys audio-thread performance; the discipline is separate from the type system.)

### SIMD and comptime

Two Zig features are directly useful. Its vector types give portable, first-class SIMD: a group of floats operated on in parallel, compiling to single SIMD instructions when they fit the target width and multiple when they do not, with graceful fallback. The min/max decimation, the cross-correlation, and the interpolation all vectorize cleanly, written once with no intrinsics and no per-target conditionals — and on the Apple-Silicon-only target this is a *single* SIMD target (128-bit NEON, uniformly present, no runtime feature detection, no scalar fallback path). Zig's compile-time evaluation is also a natural fit for specializing filter/window kernels over compile-time parameters and for compile-time asset generation (e.g., baking generated tables into the binary), which is clean in Zig and awkward elsewhere.

### The Objective-C runtime glue

Zig has no native Objective-C bridge, so talking to AppKit and Metal means going through the Objective-C runtime's C API (the `objc_msgSend` family, selector and class lookups). On Apple Silicon this is markedly less painful than it historically was, because arm64 collapses the old struct-return / float-return message-send variants into a single entry point cast per signature. This glue is real, bounded work — a modest number of message-send wrappers plus the display-link setup — and a community wrapper exists (`zig-objc`, which uses comptime to generate the casts) to shrink it. It is the tax paid for owning the whole view rather than adopting a UI framework; for a diagnostic instrument where exact frame timing matters, that ownership is arguably the right trade.

### The CLAP contract and GUI extension, at a conceptual level

Kept deliberately high-level per this document's scope: a CLAP plugin exports a single symbol; the host reaches everything else through a factory and then through *extensions* requested by string id. Every host-facing callback crosses the C ABI and must be declared with C calling convention (the most common beginner mistake, and one that manifests as a crash rather than a compile error). The plugin advertises a GUI by responding to the GUI-extension query. On macOS the host hands the plugin a parent Cocoa view through that extension, and the plugin's job is to graft its own view — hosting a Metal layer — onto it. One concrete gotcha to remember: the anonymous-union member name for the host window struct is generated by the C-import step and should be verified against what the toolchain actually emits.

### Mac-only simplifications (the compounding payoff)

Committing to one platform and one vendor removes not just code but whole categories of things that can go wrong. The ones identified, which the planning session can treat as genuine reductions in scope:

- **One audio-thread contract.** Instead of satisfying the intersection of Core Audio's, Windows' MMCSS's, and Linux's differing (and differently strict) thread rules, there is one well-documented, unusually stable contract — and the freedom to use macOS-specific mechanisms directly (`os_workgroup` to join the audio processing group, `os_unfair_lock` where a lock is truly needed, `dispatch_semaphore` for frame pacing) rather than abstracting over three implementations.
- **One SIMD target.** 128-bit NEON, uniformly present, no runtime feature detection, no dual code paths, no scalar fallback.
- **Unified memory.** The CPU and GPU share physical RAM, so getting the sample buffer to the renderer is a cache concern rather than a discrete-GPU PCIe copy. This can be *designed for* (shared storage-mode buffers) rather than merely tolerated, which a cross-platform design could never assume.
- **One of everything else:** one graphics API (the portability seam collapses to nothing built, only left room for); one shading language authored once; one windowing system (Cocoa) for the GUI handoff; one preset/state filesystem convention; one code-signing-and-notarization story; one installer format; one set of DAWs to test, all AU hosts; one binary architecture to ship (arm64, optionally fat with x86_64 as the one retained axis of variation).
- **Access to Metal's stronger capabilities.** A portable abstraction must program to the weakest target's guarantees; Metal-direct on Apple Silicon *unlocks* techniques a portable layer cannot assume (tile memory, specific threadgroup/SIMD-group behaviors, argument buffers, tight compute-render integration) — latent for the basic renderer, but relevant if the compute-based rendering variant is pursued.

### Build and packaging (conceptual)

The build produces a shared library exporting the CLAP entry symbol, linking libC and the Apple frameworks the renderer needs (Cocoa for the view, QuartzCore for the Metal layer, Metal, CoreVideo for the display link). A real build then assembles the loadable bundle the host expects (the `.clap` bundle with its `Info.plist`), runs clap-wrapper to also emit the Audio Unit `.component` for Logic, validates that with `auval`, and handles code signing and notarization (a single-platform story here, versus the separate Windows signing regime a cross-platform build would add). One practical note: shaders can be compiled at GUI-create time from an embedded Metal-source string, which avoids requiring the Metal toolchain in the build — a good fit for a Zig project with no Xcode in the loop, at the cost of a few milliseconds of one-time compile. Expect the Zig build script to need occasional adjustment across compiler versions (the shared-library API in particular has drifted).

### State of the Zig audio ecosystem (reference prior art, not dependencies)

The ecosystem is small, mostly solo developers, and should be treated as reference implementations to learn from rather than libraries to depend on. The key pieces:

- **Nakst's CLAP tutorial** is the canonical from-scratch, no-framework walkthrough of building against the raw C API. Every project below traces back to it. If the plan is to build the plugin contract by hand (recommended, given the frameworks do not help with the hard part), this is the document to work from.
- **clap-zig-bindings** (interpunct, on sourcehut) is a hand-written, idiomatic Zig translation covering 100% of the CLAP API as of version 1.2.2, explicitly *not* a framework — hand-written rather than machine-translated, so the types use Zig's type system. This is the cleanest binding path. (Its host was intermittently unreachable during the conversation, reinforcing "reference, not dependency.") Thinner C-import-based bindings also exist (SuperAuguste's, ramonmeza's).
- **Arbor** (ArborealAudio) is the one thing resembling an actual framework — comptime parameter generation, a working CLAP implementation with sample-accurate automation, a "janky" VST2 path. Its README is unusually candid and instructive: its VST3 TODO literally reads "figure out if we can write a binding without getting a lawyer" (the licensing/C++-ABI wall, confirmed by someone who hit it), and its rendering is CPU software rasterization (Olivec plus a bitmap font), with its whole GUI section a checklist of unchecked boxes. The lesson to carry: **every Zig plugin framework has punted on GPU rendering.** They do software rasterization or lean on Dear ImGui (as `zsynth` does). None has built the thing this project needs. That is not a reason to avoid Zig — it is a clarification that the framework layer is largely *irrelevant* here, because the interesting ~90% of this project is the Metal renderer, and no existing project has one to borrow.

The architecture that falls out of this ecosystem survey: hand-written CLAP bindings (or Nakst's tutorial followed directly) for the plugin contract, an owned lock-free buffer across the audio boundary, and an owned Metal view (create the Cocoa view and Metal layer, hand its pointer to the host through the CLAP GUI extension, drive it with your own Metal pipeline), skipping the entire Zig-UI-framework question. Wrap with clap-wrapper for the AU that Logic needs.

## Part 3 — The renderer as a simulation of a physical device

This section captures the *concepts* behind the rendering approach and the reasoning for them. It contains no code and prescribes no final design; it is the set of rendering ideas the conversation generated, for the planning session to adopt, adapt, or set against the alternative framings in Part 4. The central reframing is the important part.

The organizing idea is to treat the renderer not as a plotter drawing a graph but as a small **simulation of a physical device** — specifically, an analog cathode-ray oscilloscope, where an electron beam deposits energy into a phosphor that glows and slowly decays. This reframing is generative because nearly every rendering decision falls out of asking "what did the tube physically do?" rather than "how do I draw a line." Crucially, the phosphor look is not mere nostalgia: the two things it produces — brightness that varies along the trace, and a persistent halo — are direct readouts of the signal's *statistics* (where it dwells, and how much it varies) that a single-trace plot discards. The reason to reproduce the physics is that the physics *is* information, and it is cheap on a GPU.

The concepts that follow from this, as discussed:

- **A persistent, floating-point accumulation buffer.** The phosphor is one texture that survives between frames, holding accumulated glow in linear light. It must persist (the halo is the memory of recent beam positions; clearing each frame throws away the diagnostic value) and it must be floating-point for two reasons: it accumulates additively past 1.0 where the beam dwells (the high-dynamic-range "core"), and — a subtle but real point — fading correctly requires a true multiplicative decay, because an 8-bit alpha-blend-toward-black stalls once values round back to the same integer, leaving permanent ghost trails that never clear.
- **Three passes per frame over that buffer:** a *decay* pass that dims what is already there, a *trace* pass that additively deposits the new sweep's energy, and a *tonemap* pass that reads the accumulated linear energy and writes the visible, colored, gamma-encoded image.
- **Frame-rate-independent persistence.** Decay is an exponential in real elapsed time (a user-facing "persistence" time constant), so the look is identical whether the display link runs at 60, 120, or variable refresh. Short constants give a snappy modern feel; long constants give the analog storage-scope look that is excellent for watching a bassline's shape stabilize.
- **The beam as geometry, not line primitives.** Each segment between samples is expanded into an oriented quad and shaded by the fragment shader according to perpendicular distance from the centerline, giving analytic antialiasing independent of MSAA and a real beam intensity profile. Instanced rendering feeds this efficiently.
- **Velocity-weighted intensity — the concept that makes it look right.** On a real scope the beam sweeps at constant *time* rate but covers varying *screen* distance per segment; deposited brightness per unit length is therefore inversely proportional to segment screen-length. This single relationship reproduces the whole characteristic look: bright at peaks and turning points where the trace doubles back and samples pile up, dim through fast crossings where the beam races across the screen. For the target use case, a kick's slow sub-tail glows solid while its sharp click smears dim — which is exactly the information one wants foregrounded, obtained from the physics rather than designed in.
- **Bandlimited reconstruction before geometry.** The samples are not the signal; connecting them with straight lines under-draws the true continuous waveform and hides intersample peaks (the reason a file can read below full scale on a sample-peak meter yet still clip). Upsampling the window with a polyphase interpolator before building segments — the same mathematics true-peak metering uses — makes the beam follow the real bandlimited curve and makes intersample overshoots visible on the trace. Directly relevant to low-end work.
- **Tonemapping with an emergent hot core.** Compressing unbounded linear energy through a curve into displayable range and mapping through a palette that runs toward white at the top produces a white-hot core inside a colored bloom *wherever the beam dwelt*, without drawing a core explicitly — the single most recognizable feature of a good scope render, emergent from accumulation-plus-tonemap. A gradient lookup makes the palette authorable (green, amber, storage-tube blue, neutral white for measurement). Writing into an sRGB drawable keeps the palette math in linear light.
- **Buffering and threading specifics.** The accumulation textures persist and ping-pong for the decay step (not triple-buffered); only the per-frame dynamic buffers (the segment/instance data and uniforms) are triple-buffered against a small in-flight-frames semaphore, the standard Metal pattern that lets the render thread run without blocking on the GPU. The next-drawable acquisition can return nil under load and must be handled by cleanly skipping the frame.
- **The one genuine threading seam: resize.** The GUI resize callback arrives on the host main thread and must reallocate the accumulation textures the render thread is actively using. This is the single place that cannot be fully lock-free; the resolution discussed is a pending-resize flag or single-slot mailbox that the render thread services at the top of its next tick before touching the old textures. It is a handful of lines but must be gotten right, since a resize racing the renderer is a classic use-after-free or flicker that only appears when a user drags the window during playback.
- **An alternative rendering path, noted as an option.** For maximal physical fidelity (true energy splatting, variable-width blooming), segments can be rasterized in a *compute* kernel that atomically adds fixed-point energy into the accumulation buffer, giving exact control over deposition at the cost of doing one's own line rasterization and fixed-point atomics. The render-pipeline-with-additive-blending approach is more portable and looks excellent; the compute path is there if the last increment of fidelity is wanted later.

A development-sequencing philosophy specific to the renderer, worth preserving because it is how a solo graphics project keeps momentum: get the view handoff working first with a renderer that only clears the drawable to a dim color (proving the whole CLAP-GUI-plus-Objective-C-plus-wrapper chain end to end, so it never needs debugging again); then a single dim aliased trace (proving the history buffer end to end); then the persistent float buffer and decay (the thing gains memory and feels alive); then velocity weighting (it stops looking like a plot); then bandlimited upsampling and the hot-core tonemap (it starts looking like hardware). Each step is independently visible and independently satisfying — never more than an afternoon from a better screenshot.

## Part 4 — The functionality: the whole sprawling array

This is the heart of the handoff and the part to keep *unsynthesized*. The brainstorm deliberately refused to narrow "an oscilloscope" into one product, for a specific reason that should frame everything below.

### The reframing that generated the array

The word "oscilloscope" silently smuggles in three commitments — a single trace, amplitude against *linear time* as the axes, and the *present moment* as privileged. None of these are facts about signals. They are facts about what a 1950s cathode-ray tube could physically do with one electron gun and a sweep generator. The whole thread's earlier arc was about escaping the *look* of old hardware; this part is about escaping its *epistemology* — the assumptions about what to show and how to lay it out. The generative move is to stop starting from "scope" and start from the *questions a producer actually has*, then choose for each question the geometry that answers it most directly. Very few of the real questions are naturally "amplitude versus linear time as one curve." The unifying principle that emerged: **the signal is one thing; the representations are lenses; the instrument's job is to make swapping lenses fluid**, so you reach for the geometry that matches your question instead of contorting your question to fit the one geometry the hardware bequeathed.

What follows is the full menu of lenses discussed, in two families — the faithful/traditional monitors, and the framings that break one or more of the tube's three assumptions. Present tense throughout; none of these is chosen over the others.

### Family A — Faithful and traditional monitors

- **The phosphor oscilloscope** itself: the velocity-weighted, persistent, hot-cored trace of Part 3. Optimal for a narrow but genuinely important class of questions — the exact shape of a single transient, spotting a lone glitch, seeing a discontinuity — and it survives every attempt to replace it wholesale precisely because for those questions amplitude-against-linear-time *is* the right representation.
- **Musically-aware triggering**, which is where a software scope can beat a hardware one rather than merely imitate it. A hardware scope triggers on a voltage threshold because that is all it has; a plugin knows the host transport and much more. Three trigger modes discussed: **host-transport-locked sweep** (trigger every quarter note, sixteenth, or bar, with an adjustable offset, so periodic musical material stands still and a kick's waveform can be watched evolving across many bars without touching a trigger control); **transient-detection trigger** (an envelope-derivative or spectral-flux detector that locks onto a kick attack reliably, where a zero-crossing threshold fails because a sub-heavy kick crosses zero several times before its transient); and **pitch-locked sweep** (detect the fundamental, set the sweep period to an integer multiple of it, so a sustained bass freezes into a stable waveform).
- **A measurement layer** of the kind hardware scopes have had for decades and software has largely abandoned: droppable cursors giving time and amplitude deltas, with readouts that are *musically literate* — expressing a time delta simultaneously in milliseconds, samples, degrees of phase at a specified reference frequency, and note values at the project tempo. The phase reading is the number that actually matters for kick-versus-bass alignment, because in the overlap region (roughly 50–70 Hz for progressive material) a 2 ms offset is nearly a quarter cycle, the difference between reinforcement and partial cancellation — a fact producers currently arrive at by nudging and listening.
- **The comparison / alignment workflow**, the direct analogue of an EQ showing other instances' curves and arguably the feature that would justify the whole project. Drop the plugin on the kick channel and the bass channel; have the instances discover each other and share buffers using the host playhead's sample position as a common clock (which is automatically immune to differing plugin-delay compensation on the two chains). Then overlay the two traces in different colors, show their sum as a third, and show a live cross-correlation curve over a range of candidate offsets, its peak marking the alignment that maximizes low-end summation and a second marker showing the polarity-inverted alternative. Instead of trial and error, the producer sees the whole solution space and can drag the offset to watch the summed low end grow or collapse. This is the daily bread of every kick-bass genre and no one has built the visual, musical, interactive version.
- **Stereo monitoring done right.** The bouncing-needle correlation meter is nearly useless because a single scalar that jumps around does not say *when* the problem occurred; the better form is a **correlation-history plot on the same time axis as the waveform, ideally per frequency band**, so a low-end mono-compatibility problem appears as a band of red at a specific moment. Paired with a proper **X-Y vectorscope** using the same phosphor accumulation, this gives the stereo picture in a form one can reason about.
- **True-peak / intersample-overshoot display**, following from the bandlimited reconstruction: the trace shows where the limiter is producing overshoots above a reference line, seen rather than merely reported.
- **Navigation and comparison affordances** that RAM makes trivial and hardware never had: a deep rolling capture buffer to scroll backward when something odd is heard (rather than trying to reproduce it), snapshot layers to freeze and overlay against the live signal for A/B work, full-screen mode, and a genuinely resizable vector interface throughout.

### Family B — Framings freed from the tube

Each of these breaks one or more of the three inherited assumptions.

- **Delay embedding / phase-space attractor** (drops linear time entirely; the framing flagged as the most different and most fun). From the scalar stream, form vectors of the signal and its delayed copies and plot the path traced — a trajectory in state space that, per Takens' theorem, reconstructs the geometry of the process generating the sound rather than its amplitude over time. What makes it musically real: a pure sine traces a clean closed ellipse; a harmonically rich periodic tone traces a more intricate but still *closed* loop whose shape is the waveshape made geometric (its symmetry reporting even-versus-odd harmonic balance, its complexity reporting spectral richness); two incommensurate tones never close and wind around a torus; noise fills the space; pitch drift shows as the loop slowly precessing; nonlinear distortion warps the attractor's shape in a way one can watch while turning up the drive. Choosing the delay from the detected pitch makes the loop sit still. This generalizes the century-old vectorscope and Lissajous trick (themselves the degenerate two-dimensional case, left-against-right) into a first-class way of seeing timbre and stability as *form*. It maps cleanly onto the Part 3 renderer, since a phase-space trajectory is still just a path splatted into a density buffer. An open sub-task noted: work out sensible embedding parameters (delay, dimension) for audio and how the delay tracks detected pitch.
- **Time-frequency as the primary instrument** (dissolves the historical accident that put scope and spectrum analyzer in separate boxes; they are Fourier duals, two marginals of one richer object). Music lives in the joint plane — a note *is* a frequency persisting over a duration (a horizontal stroke), a transient *is* energy spread across frequency at a moment (a vertical one). Make the time-frequency surface primary and let the waveform and the instantaneous spectrum be slices pulled off it. The modern methods are computationally lavish in exactly the way an Apple-Silicon GPU rewards: a **constant-Q or wavelet transform** gives sharp time resolution up high and sharp frequency resolution down low (matching logarithmic pitch and broadband transients, unlike the uniform Gabor-limit blur of a plain spectrogram), and **reassignment / synchrosqueezing** relocates each smear of energy to its center of gravity so partials snap into crisp ridges. For the target use case: a reassigned constant-Q view of the 20–200 Hz region on a log-frequency axis shows the kick's pitch-drop as a descending curve, the bass note as a horizontal line, and their *interference* — the beating and masking where they collide — as visible structure in the shared band. That directly answers "do these two fight in the low end," which the amplitude trace cannot express.
- **Statistical density done honestly** (reclaims the phosphor's soul while discarding its body). The persistence glow was always a sloppy density estimate; owning that lets one do it correctly and better — accumulate a true normalized histogram of where the trajectory goes and read it as an explicit probability density. The mature form is the **eye diagram** from digital communications: overlay many symbol-periods aligned to the symbol clock, and the spread shows jitter and noise directly. The audio translation: align many cycles to the detected pitch period, or many bars to the tempo grid, and overlay them; a steady bass produces a tight sharp "eye," a drifting one a fuzzy one, with the fuzziness *quantified*. One can draw the mean cycle as a bright line and a ±σ band around it — which no phosphor could do, because a phosphor cannot compute a standard deviation. Keeps everything that made persistence feel alive and replaces the accidental physics with statistics locked to a musically meaningful clock.
- **Dropping the privileged present** (the "now" assumption). With available RAM there is no reason the instrument shows only the present; treat the whole track as a continuously recorded time-frequency-amplitude record that one scrubs and zooms through at any scale from single sample to entire arrangement, map-like, with the live view merely the right edge of an infinite scroll. This dissolves the trigger problem completely — one never freezes the signal, one navigates a persistent record of it. The tradeoff, stated honestly: it changes the tool from an *instrument* into something closer to a *document*.
- **Depth / waterfall** (a 3D surface receding into the screen). Good for grasping how a sound evolves as a whole; bad for reading exact values, because perspective and occlusion are the enemies of measurement. Flagged with explicit caution: the honest place for depth is as a complement for insight, never as the surface one takes a number off of. The general failure mode to avoid across all of Family B is chasing novelty into representations that look striking and measure worse — a measurement instrument that sacrifices measurement has lost the plot.

### The synthesis *principle* (not a product decision)

Stated as a principle for the planning session to weigh, not as a chosen design: the mistake was never the trace — it is the best answer to the transient-shape questions and should be kept for them. The mistake is building an instrument that offers *only* the trace, or in the modern degenerate case only the trace plus one blurry spectrogram. An instrument designed from the questions would be *plural*: keep the velocity-weighted trace for transient questions; let the same underlying signal pivot into a phase-space attractor for judging timbre and stability, into a reassigned time-frequency surface for judging pitch and masking, into an aligned statistical density for judging steadiness, and into a relational cross-view for judging two signals against each other. The unifying design value is fluid lens-swapping over one signal.

### The recurring concrete use case

The kick-and-bass low-end alignment problem is the throughline and a good stress test for any subset chosen. The "ideal freed-from-the-tube" instrument for it almost designs itself under the plurality principle: a shared low-frequency time-frequency canvas with kick and bass overlaid so their collision is seen directly; a phase-space loop per signal so the bass's waveshape can be watched holding or wandering; and the alignment cross-correlation not as a bolted-on feature but as one more lens on the same two signals. None of that is a scope in the CRT sense, and all of it answers the questions actually being asked.
